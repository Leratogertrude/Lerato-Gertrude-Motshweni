### Reflection


### Situation
We were a four-person student team building **CareerCraft**, an AI-supported career platform for South African graduates.
The assessment required an Agile Team Charter as a social contract: purpose, roles, ceremonies, working agreements and a Definition of Done. 
At the same time, the product idea was still broad, roles overlapped, and later sprints showed that a document written at the start would
not automaticallymatch how we actually worked.

### Task
My task was to use the charter as evidence of how the team agreed to work, then reflect on whether those agreements helped or failed in real sprints.
I needed to show learning, not only copy the template.

### Action
I selected the charter because it records why the team exists, who owns what, how we communicate, and when work is really done.
I compared it with the logbook. Initiation Week 4 was used to document roles and ceremonies. 
Sprint 4 showed the practical problem: some responsibilities overlapped and some backlog items were solution statements rather than user stories,
so we had to rewrite items and assign owners. Sprint 5 tested the working agreements when lecturer feedback said the prototype was too dark. 
Instead of defending the first design, the team changed the palette and reviewed accessibility. I
also noted gaps in the charter itself: decision-making boxes were unticked, the document mentioned two-week sprints while delivery used shorter cycles,
and the success line named “Youth Catalyst Lab” while the product is CareerCraft.

### Result
The charter helped the team speak the same language about purpose, stand-ups, reviews and quality. 
The Definition of Done made it clearer that a screen is not finished until it is reviewed, accepted and safe.
The result of using it critically was also honesty: a charter only works if it is kept alive.
I learned to separate **role titles** from **task ownership**, to raise blockers early, and to treat feedback as part of the agreement.
Next time I would tick one decision-making method, write the real sprint length, keep the product name consistent, 
and review the charter after the first sprints rather than leaving it as a start-of-project file.

## Reflection 1: Agile Team Charter

### Situation

Our team needed a shared agreement to guide how we would work together on
CareerCraft, a project intended to help students and graduates prepare for
employment. The charter set out our purpose, team roles, communication
channels, working agreements and approach to Scrum.

### Task

We needed to establish clear responsibilities and ways of working so that
the team could coordinate its efforts, address blockers and deliver work
that supported our shared vision.

### Action

I reviewed the team’s purpose, roles and working agreements and considered
how they would support our project. The charter identified Livhuwani as
Product Owner, Lwandile as Agile Lead, and Sello and Lerato as contributors
to development, analysis and testing. It also outlined stand-ups, sprint
planning, reviews and retrospectives, as well as the use of Teams,
WhatsApp and email for communication.

### Result

The charter gave us a foundation for assigning responsibilities and
communicating as a team. However, some tasks still overlapped between
roles, showing me that written roles need to be supported by clear task
ownership and regular discussion.

I also noticed that the original charter referred to Youth Catalyst Lab
and deliverables that did not fully match the later CareerCraft concept.
I learned that a charter should be revisited as a project evolves. In
future, I would review it during retrospectives, update its scope and
Definition of Done, and clarify how decisions are made.

### Reflection

### Situation
I am **Lerato Motshweni**, Developer / Analyst / Tester.
The Trello product backlog had **50** cards. Some still say **Youth Catalyst Lab**,
which was our old name before **CareerCraft**. Cards also mix product features (templates, portal, AI) with process
work (stand-ups) and later launch work (October pilot). My risk was treating that column as a dump instead of an ordered 
backlog.

### Task
My task was to evidence the real board, explain the name change, 
show how items were split into tasks, and reflect on my analyst/tester role in ranking and refining work.

### Action
I screenshot the **PRODUCT BACKLOG** column and opened complete cards rather than inventing extra items.

I used three cards as my working examples:

- **CV Template Design: Design & Arts (3 Sep)** has my **L** avatar.
The description ties the template to Pnet stats and ATS rules. The checklist is the breakdown I care about as a tester:
research aesthetics, build ATS templates, test with sample portfolios, get Livhuwani’s approval. 
That is a product-backlog item turned into tasks.
- **Beta-Testing Workshops** shows validation on the backlog, not only build work: recruit testers, 
walk through the portal, collect AI-prompt feedback, log bugs on the Blocked list.
- **Final System QA** kept regression, MFA/security and payment success/failure on the list as **High Priority**, 
so quality was not an afterthought.

I also left process cards visible. **Daily Stand-up & Board Management Rituals (19 Jun)** is Lwandile’s recurring card.
It is not a user feature, but it is how the backlog was walked **right to left** every day. 
That matches our charter: 15-minute stand-up, 4-hour response rule, clear blockers.

For naming, I did not edit history.
**Portal: Youth Catalyst Lab Web Build** stays in the screenshot. In this portfolio I say the product is CareerCraft.

I did **not** mark **Final Project Report & Velocity Review (1/4)** as complete. 
October cards stay as later product-backlog work, not as June–September sprint completions.

### Result
The evidence is Trello: column + card checklists + owners. I learned that a backlog is useful when:

- old names are explained, not hidden  
- a feature card has tasks I can pass or fail  
- research, DoD, workshops and QA sit next to build cards  
- unfinished work stays unfinished  

If I did this again I would keep CareerCraft as the only product name on new cards, pin DoR/DoD on the list header, and keep Must items at the top of Trello so the board rank matches the real order. I do not need more cards. I need this board, these four card close-ups, and an honest STAR reflection

### Reflection

### Situation

During Sprint 5 of the CareerCraft project, our team focused on designing
the core user experience in Figma. After presenting the initial prototype,
we received feedback that its dark theme was difficult to read.

### Task

The team needed to revise the prototype and prepare it for a follow-up
demonstration. My task was to research colour contrast and accessible palette
guidance so that the revised design would be easier to read.

### Action

I researched colour contrast and contributed to a colour-contrast reference
guide. The team divided the remaining work across the sprint backlog: members
updated the job-matching, profile, CV builder and dashboard screens, while
we worked together to consolidate the prototype and review the changes.

### Result

The team produced a brighter prototype and completed an internal walkthrough.
The sprint backlog helped make the selected work and responsibilities clear,
while the contrast guide supported a more consistent approach to the redesign.

I learned that a sprint backlog should be flexible enough to respond to
feedback while keeping the team focused on its sprint goal. In future, I
would check accessibility earlier in the design process and make sure that
related tasks and acceptance criteria are clear before implementation.
