---
### Reflection


### Situation
We were a four-person student team building **CareerCraft**, an AI-supported career platform for South African graduates.
The assessment required an Agile Team Charter as a social contract: purpose, roles, ceremonies, working agreements and a Definition of Done. 
At the same time, the product idea was still broad, roles overlapped, and later sprints showed that a document written at the start would
not automaticallymatch how we actually worked.

### Task
My task was to use the charter as evidence of how the team agreed to work, then reflect on whether those agreements helped or failed in real sprints.
I needed to show learning, not only copy the template.

### Action
I selected the charter because it records why the team exists, who owns what, how we communicate, and when work is really done.
I compared it with the logbook. Initiation Week 4 was used to document roles and ceremonies. 
Sprint 4 showed the practical problem: some responsibilities overlapped and some backlog items were solution statements rather than user stories,
so we had to rewrite items and assign owners. Sprint 5 tested the working agreements when lecturer feedback said the prototype was too dark. 
Instead of defending the first design, the team changed the palette and reviewed accessibility. I
also noted gaps in the charter itself: decision-making boxes were unticked, the document mentioned two-week sprints while delivery used shorter cycles,
and the success line named “Youth Catalyst Lab” while the product is CareerCraft.

### Result
The charter helped the team speak the same language about purpose, stand-ups, reviews and quality. 
The Definition of Done made it clearer that a screen is not finished until it is reviewed, accepted and safe.
The result of using it critically was also honesty: a charter only works if it is kept alive.
I learned to separate **role titles** from **task ownership**, to raise blockers early, and to treat feedback as part of the agreement.
Next time I would tick one decision-making method, write the real sprint length, keep the product name consistent, 
and review the charter after the first sprints rather than leaving it as a start-of-project file.
